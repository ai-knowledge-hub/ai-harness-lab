# **Phase 3 — Curriculum and lesson architecture**

**Objective:** establish a coherent educational architecture for the Algorithms & Data track, define how individual Context modules should teach, and determine how the track connects to the wider Harness curriculum.

In Phase 2, we identified a recurring pattern across the 15 mechanisms: their greatest educational value lies not in their individual implementations, but in the computational principles they reveal.

Binary Search teaches elimination through structure. Heaps teach the value of partial ordering. Bloom Filters teach the economics of uncertainty. Leases teach the limits of ownership in distributed systems.

Phase 3 is where we turn these observations into a curriculum.

I want to make an important distinction before we proceed. **A curriculum is not necessarily a sequence of pages that everyone must read in order.** It is a deliberately constructed network of knowledge, with clear conceptual dependencies and multiple entry points.

Harness should support both the reader who arrives wanting to understand a particular algorithm and the reader who wants to develop a systematic understanding of computer science.

## **1. The intellectual identity of the track**

### **From algorithms to computational reasoning**

Most traditional algorithm courses are organised around a taxonomy of computational techniques: sorting, searching, graph algorithms, greedy algorithms, dynamic programming and so on.

This is a perfectly legitimate approach, particularly for formal computer science education. Harness, however, has a slightly different mission.

We want readers to understand how technology systems work, why their architectures exist and how their behaviour follows from underlying constraints.

That suggests a complementary approach.

| Traditional algorithm learning      | Harness computational learning                                             |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| How do I implement binary search?   | What properties allow a computer to eliminate possibilities?               |
| How do I implement a heap?          | How much order does a system actually need?                                |
| How do I traverse a graph?          | How does a system explore relationships it has not yet discovered?         |
| How does a Bloom filter work?       | When is approximate knowledge more valuable than exact knowledge?          |
| How do I implement a state machine? | How can a system constrain its own behaviour?                              |
| How does a distributed lock work?   | How can independent processes coordinate when ownership becomes uncertain? |

The questions on the right do not replace those on the left. They establish the reason for learning them.

### **Proposed editorial mission**

The Algorithms & Data track explores the computational ideas beneath modern technology systems.

Through interactive experiments, mathematical reasoning and real implementations, readers learn how computers represent information, eliminate unnecessary work, navigate relationships, allocate resources and coordinate behaviour.

The aim is not simply to recognise or implement an algorithm, but to understand the conditions that make it useful, the guarantees it provides and the trade-offs that determine whether it belongs in a particular system.

I would use this as the governing editorial brief for the track rather than necessarily publishing it verbatim.

## **3. The shared foundations layer**

One of the findings from Phase 2 was that several important concepts recur across almost every mechanism.

If we explain them independently in each Context module, we risk repetition. If we assume readers already understand them, we risk creating an unnecessarily high barrier to entry.

I propose a shared introductory lesson.

### **Proposed lesson: How Computers Turn Problems Into Computation**

This would be a short foundational guide, supported by small experiments rather than another large algorithm visualizer.

Its purpose is to establish five concepts.

### **3.1 Representation — what does the computer actually see?**

A computer does not inherently understand a website, an advertising campaign, a road network or a business transaction.

We choose a representation that preserves the properties relevant to our problem.

### **One problem, different representations**

Illustrative website data

**Sequence**

Preserves an ordering of pages.

**Graph**

Preserves relationships between pages.

The same underlying entities support different computational operations depending on how they are represented.

This establishes why representation is an engineering decision rather than merely a programming detail.

### **3.2 Computational cost — what does efficiency mean?**

Introduce input size, primitive operations, time complexity, space complexity and asymptotic growth.

Rather than beginning with formal Big-O notation, begin with the consequences of increasing input size.

### **Experiment: how computation grows**

Illustrative growth functions on a logarithmic bar scale. These are not measured operation counts or execution times.

The reader should understand that complexity describes how computational requirements grow, not how many milliseconds a program will take.

We can subsequently introduce worst-case, expected and amortised complexity when individual lessons make those distinctions necessary.

### **3.3 Invariants — why does a mechanism remain correct?**

An invariant is a property that remains true at the relevant stages of execution.

A heap preserves a parent–child ordering property. Binary Search preserves the region in which an unresolved answer could exist. A state machine preserves constraints on legal transitions when those transitions are correctly enforced.

This concept should become one of the recurring intellectual signatures of the track.

### **3.4 Assumptions — what must be true before the mechanism works?**

Every algorithm operates over a model of the problem.

Dijkstra requires non-negative edge weights. Boundary-finding binary search requires a monotonic predicate. A standard Bloom filter's no-false-negative guarantee depends on correct maintenance of its insertion state.

An implementation can be faithful to an algorithm and still be unsuitable for the problem it has been given.

### **3.5 Guarantees — what can we conclude after execution?**

We should teach readers to distinguish exact results, approximate results, conditional guarantees and system-level outcomes.

This prepares them for later lessons on distributed systems, where correctness is rarely a property of one isolated function.

### **Scope decision**

I would keep this foundations lesson relatively compact, approximately 1,200–1,800 words with a few interactive demonstrations.

We should not turn it into a mandatory computer science prerequisite course. Readers should be able to consult individual concepts as they encounter them in the labs.

## **4. Curriculum progression and conceptual dependencies**

The seven conceptual groups establish the intellectual structure. We also need to understand which lessons depend on others.

For example, Dijkstra becomes much easier to understand once the reader has encountered both graph traversal and priority queues.

Topological Sort builds on graph representation and traversal. Lease-Based Ownership becomes more meaningful once the reader understands state transitions and concurrency.

### **Conceptual dependency map**

Proposed learning dependencies, not mandatory navigation paths.

The map represents broad conceptual progression rather than formal mathematical prerequisites. For instance, understanding Bloom Filters is not required to understand token buckets, and audit hash chains can be studied independently of authorisation models.

During implementation, we should encode only genuine prerequisites and useful related concepts.

### **Proposed learning paths**

Rather than requiring everyone to follow the entire curriculum, we can provide several paths based on what the reader wants to understand.

## **5. Architecture of an individual lesson**

This is the most important deliverable of Phase 3 because it establishes how we will rewrite the existing Context modules.

The architecture should follow the general Harness teaching progression:

**Concrete problem → First principles → Engineering mechanism → Real implementation → Application → Test.**

However, interactive algorithm lessons introduce an additional opportunity: readers can move repeatedly between explanation and experimentation.

I propose the following lesson model.

01

**Encounter the problem**

*Why is the straightforward approach insufficient?*

A concrete scenario with a measurable or observable constraint.

02

**Discover the principle**

*What property of the problem can we exploit?*

The relevant mathematical or computational idea.

03

**Construct the mechanism**

*How does that property become an algorithm?*

State, operations, representation and invariant.

04

**Observe execution**

*Can I see why each step is necessary?*

A guided experiment using the Run tab.

05

**Account for the cost**

*What work has been saved, and what does it cost?*

Complexity, resource requirements and alternatives.

06

**Challenge the guarantee**

*What happens when the assumptions stop holding?*

A counterexample or failure scenario.

07

**Enter a real system**

*How does an actual implementation use this mechanism?*

One or two verified implementation examples.

08

**Transfer the knowledge**

*Can I recognise this problem somewhere else?*

An unfamiliar application and a reasoning exercise.

### **The architecture should be flexible**

We should not mechanically impose eight headings on every lesson.

Binary Search may need considerable attention to its correctness argument but relatively little discussion of implementation architecture.

Bloom Filters may need more mathematical experimentation.

Lease-Based Ownership may require a longer failure narrative and almost no conventional Big-O analysis.

The structure defines the educational responsibilities of a lesson, not its visible table of contents.

### **Proposed depth by mechanism type**

| Lesson type             | Primary emphasis                                   | Suggested Context length |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Classical algorithm     | Representation, invariant, correctness, complexity | 800–1,300 words          |
| Data structure          | Operations, representation, memory and trade-offs  | 900–1,400 words          |
| Probabilistic mechanism | Probability, uncertainty and resource trade-offs   | 1,000–1,600 words        |
| Coordination mechanism  | State, timing, concurrency and failure             | 1,200–1,800 words        |
| Security mechanism      | Threat model, enforcement and trust boundaries     | 1,000–1,600 words        |

These are working editorial ranges, not quotas. We should calibrate them against the actual Context interface during the pilot.

## **6. Redesigning the relationship between Run and Context**

I would avoid treating Context as documentation that sits beside the animation.

Instead, the two tabs should form a single learning experience.

The Context explains what to notice. The Run tab makes that idea observable. The reader then returns to the Context with a stronger mental model.

### **Proposed learning loop**

There are three levels of interactive learning we should aim to support.

| Level      | Reader activity                            | Learning outcome                    |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------- |
| Observe    | Step through a prepared execution          | Recognise the mechanism             |
| Predict    | Anticipate the next state before advancing | Understand the mechanism            |
| Experiment | Change an input or assumption              | Evaluate when the mechanism applies |

Most algorithm visualizers are effective at the first level. Harness should deliberately develop the second and third.

### **A concrete example: Binary Search**

Rather than simply explaining that binary search halves the search space, we can invite the reader to predict what will happen when the ordering assumption changes.

## **7. The role of mathematics**

I think this deserves explicit editorial consideration.

Harness has an opportunity to introduce mathematics as a language for explaining engineering decisions rather than presenting it as a separate academic subject.

We should neither avoid mathematics to make lessons accessible nor introduce formalism before the reader understands the problem it describes.

### **Proposed progression**

First · Intuition

Each binary search comparison eliminates approximately half the remaining possibilities.

Then · Observable pattern

1,024 → 512 → 256 → 128 → 64 → … → 1

Then · Mathematical relationship

\\[ \frac{n}{2^k}\leq 1 \\]

\\[ k\geq \log_2 n \\]

Finally · Engineering implication

Doubling the size of an ordered, randomly accessible search space adds approximately one halving step.

The mathematics emerges from the reader's investigation.

This approach can be applied throughout the curriculum.

| Mechanism        | Mathematical idea                | Engineering implication       |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Binary Search    | Logarithms                       | Search-space reduction        |
| Heap             | Tree height and partial ordering | Efficient priority operations |
| BFS / DFS        | Graph theory                     | Relationship exploration      |
| Union-Find       | Equivalence relations            | Incremental connectivity      |
| Bloom Filter     | Probability                      | Approximate membership        |
| Circular Buffer  | Modular arithmetic               | Reusable bounded storage      |
| Rabin–Karp       | Polynomial hashing               | Reusable computation          |
| Topological Sort | Partial orders                   | Dependency constraints        |
| Dijkstra         | Weighted graphs and optimality   | Cost-aware routing            |
| Token Bucket     | Rates and accumulation           | Resource allocation over time |
| State Machine    | Sets and transition relations    | Behavioural constraints       |
| RBAC / ABAC      | Relations and predicates         | Policy decisions              |
| Leases           | Temporal validity and ordering   | Distributed ownership         |
| Audit Hash Chain | Cryptographic commitments        | Detectable modification       |

We should derive mathematical expressions where they materially improve understanding. For results requiring substantial prerequisites, such as Union-Find's inverse Ackermann complexity bound, we can explain the significance and provide an optional deeper treatment rather than pretending to prove the result in a few paragraphs.

## **8. Integrating real implementations**

One of the risks in enriching the Context modules is that we turn each one into a catalogue of possible applications.

For example, a Heap lesson could list operating systems, search engines, databases, AI retrieval, advertising auctions and job schedulers. That would make the lesson appear comprehensive without necessarily teaching anything new.

I propose a more selective evidence model.

### **Three levels of application evidence**

| Level                     | Evidence requirement                                    | Example                                                            |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Illustrative application  | Clearly labelled hypothetical scenario                  | A campaign tracker maintains the ten largest observed spend values |
| Documented implementation | Official documentation or source code                   | Python `heapq` implements binary heap operations                   |
| System-level application  | Evidence of how the mechanism fits into a larger system | Investigate a database's actual bounded-sort implementation        |

Each lesson should normally contain one strong documented implementation and, where useful, one system-level case.

The illustrative running example remains valuable because it gives us control over the problem's complexity. The documented example then shows where the simplified model meets real engineering.

These are candidates for the evidence phase, not newly verified production claims.

We should not simplify the production system until it falsely resembles the algorithm we are teaching.

## **9. Proposed architecture for all 15 Context modules**

We can now establish the editorial brief for each individual lesson.

These are proposed titles and lesson directions, not final approved copy.

### **Group I — Structure and elimination**

**01. Binary Search — How Much Can One Comparison Tell Us?**

Central question

When can a computer safely eliminate half of the possible answers?

Running example

Finding the first point at which cumulative expenditure reaches a budget threshold.

Intellectual outcome

Understand monotonicity, search invariants and logarithmic growth.

Boundary experiment

Replace cumulative expenditure with a fluctuating performance metric.

### **Group II — Selective ordering and reuse**

**02. Heap — Why Sorting Everything Is Sometimes Wasteful**

Central question

How much ordering must a system maintain to retrieve its most important results?

Running example

Maintaining the highest-spending campaigns in a stream of fixed-score records.

Intellectual outcome

Understand partial ordering, priority queues and bounded selection.

Boundary experiment

Allow previously discarded candidates to change their scores.

**03. Rabin–Karp — How Computers Avoid Repeating the Same Work**

Central question

How can overlapping computations share their previous results?

Running example

Searching a stream of document content for a known string.

Intellectual outcome

Understand rolling hashes, reusable computation and candidate verification.

Boundary experiment

Construct different strings with matching hash values.

### **Group III — Relationships and exploration**

**04. Breadth-First Search — How to Explore an Unknown Network**

Central question

How can a system discover reachable entities in increasing distance from a starting point?

Running example

Exploring links between pages on a website.

Intellectual outcome

Understand graph representation, frontiers and shortest unweighted paths.

Boundary experiment

Introduce cycles and weighted edges.

**05. Depth-First Search — How Computers Explore and Backtrack**

Central question

How does a computer explore one possibility while remembering the alternatives?

Running example

Exploring a repository's directory or dependency structure.

Intellectual outcome

Understand stacks, recursion, backtracking and traversal state.

Boundary experiment

Introduce a directed cycle and a very deep branch.

**06. Union-Find — How Systems Discover What Belongs Together**

Central question

How can a system maintain connected groups without repeatedly traversing the entire network?

Running example

Grouping infrastructure incidents according to observed relationships.

Intellectual outcome

Understand equivalence classes, path compression and amortised computation.

Boundary experiment

Add an incorrect relationship, then attempt to remove it.

### **Group IV — Dependencies and optimisation**

**07. Topological Sort — Why Some Work Must Wait**

Central question

How can a system discover which tasks are eligible to execute?

Running example

A reporting pipeline with several independently produced inputs.

Intellectual outcome

Understand partial orders, dependency constraints and parallel readiness.

Boundary experiment

Introduce a dependency cycle and limited worker capacity.

**08. Dijkstra — When the Cheapest Next Step Is Not the Cheapest Journey**

Central question

Under what conditions can local decisions establish a globally optimal path?

Running example

Routing through a network with fixed, non-negative costs.

Intellectual outcome

Understand weighted graphs, relaxation and greedy correctness.

Boundary experiment

Introduce negative weights or changing costs.

### **Group V — Finite resources and uncertainty**

**09. Bloom Filter — When Approximate Knowledge Is Good Enough**

Central question

How much certainty can a system afford to sacrifice to reduce memory and lookup costs?

Running example

Screening URL membership before consulting an exact index.

Intellectual outcome

Understand probabilistic membership and asymmetric error guarantees.

Boundary experiment

Increase filter occupancy until false positives become frequent.

**10. Circular Buffer — How Computers Remember Without Remembering Everything**

Central question

How can a system retain recent information using fixed storage?

Running example

Maintaining a bounded history of telemetry events.

Intellectual outcome

Understand logical versus physical ordering, modular arithmetic and retention.

Boundary experiment

Compare overwrite, reject and block policies when the buffer fills.

**11. Token Bucket — Why Systems Allow Bursts but Limit Sustained Demand**

Central question

How can a system distribute limited processing permission over time?

Running example

Controlling requests to an external API.

Intellectual outcome

Understand burst capacity, refill rates and throughput guarantees.

Boundary experiment

Increase request duration while keeping admission rate constant.

### **Group VI — State and coordination**

**12. State Machine — How Systems Prevent Actions From Happening in the Wrong Order**

Central question

How can a system define and enforce permitted changes in behaviour?

Running example

An operation requiring approval before external execution.

Intellectual outcome

Understand states, events, guards, transitions and atomic enforcement.

Boundary experiment

Allow two workers to attempt the same transition concurrently.

**13. Lease-Based Ownership — How Systems Recover Abandoned Work**

Central question

How can independent workers coordinate when ownership may expire?

Running example

Recovering a job from an apparently unresponsive worker.

Intellectual outcome

Understand temporary authority, recovery, stale execution and fencing.

Boundary experiment

Resume the original worker after another worker acquires ownership.

### **Group VII — Authority and trust**

**14. Access Control — How Systems Decide Who May Do What**

Central question

How can a system derive an authorisation decision from identities, relationships and policy?

Running example

Determining whether an analyst may access a client's report.

Intellectual outcome

Understand role-based, attribute-based and relationship-based decisions.

Boundary experiment

Change resource ownership or contextual policy while keeping the user's role unchanged.

**15. Audit Hash Chain — How Systems Make Changes to History Detectable**

Central question

What evidence allows a system to detect modification of previously recorded information?

Running example

Maintaining a verifiable history of approved operations.

Intellectual outcome

Understand cryptographic commitments, integrity and independent trust anchors.

Boundary experiment

Modify an event and recompute the entire subsequent chain.

Notice that the proposed titles express the computational problem rather than simply repeating the mechanism's name.

I would nevertheless retain the conventional algorithm names prominently in navigation, search metadata and lesson headings. Readers should not have to guess which lesson contains Dijkstra or a Bloom Filter.

## **10. Visual and interaction architecture**

We should establish a common visual language across the module without forcing every mechanism into an identical visual representation.

### **Consistent visual semantics**

### **Proposed shared visual vocabulary**

**Available information**

Unprocessed nodes, records or candidates.

**Active operation**

The current comparison, transition or decision.

**Established result**

A settled distance, retained candidate or verified state.

**Inactive information**

Eliminated candidates or irrelevant state.

**Violation or failure**

An invalid assumption, rejected operation or broken guarantee.

Illustrative semantic palette. Final colours should follow the existing Harness design system and accessibility requirements. Colour must not be the only indicator of state.

If an amber element represents the current operation in Binary Search, it should not represent a completed operation in another lab without a clear reason.

Consistency reduces the cognitive effort required to interpret each new animation.

### **Different mechanisms need different visual models**

| Mechanism family         | Preferred visual representation                      |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Ordered search           | Sequences, intervals and boundaries                  |
| Graph algorithms         | Nodes, edges, frontiers and distances                |
| Heaps and Union-Find     | Trees with underlying array or parent representation |
| Hashing                  | Input transformations and bit or hash state          |
| Resource management      | Capacity, occupancy and time                         |
| State machines           | Transition diagrams and event histories              |
| Distributed coordination | Parallel process timelines                           |
| Security and integrity   | Policy relationships and evidence chains             |

A distributed lease is particularly poorly served by a simple sequential animation. Its defining failure emerges from the interaction of multiple timelines.

Conversely, a timeline would add unnecessary complexity to a straightforward binary search demonstration.

The visual representation should follow the structure of the problem.

### **What every visualizer should expose**

The current state, the operation being performed, the reason for that operation, and the property that must remain true.

For more advanced mechanisms, it should also expose the assumptions under which the displayed behaviour is valid.

This is a stronger educational standard than highlighting lines of code while an animation progresses.

## **13. Establishing the editorial production contract**

As we move into Phase 4, we need a consistent specification for each lesson.

This is particularly useful because the eventual repository implementation may be performed in a separate coding-agent workflow.

The editorial deliverable for each mechanism should contain the following:

| Deliverable             | Contents                                                            |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Lesson brief            | Central question, audience, prerequisites, learning objectives      |
| Evidence register       | Primary sources, implementation references, claim-to-source mapping |
| Technical specification | Input model, operations, invariant, guarantees, assumptions         |
| Context copy            | Complete reviewed lesson text                                       |
| Visual brief            | Learning objective, states, transitions, scenarios                  |
| Implementation review   | Required changes to code, data and animation, if any                |
| Transfer exercise       | Question, expected reasoning and explanation                        |
| Integration brief       | Related lessons and justified cross-links                           |
| Approval status         | Draft, technically reviewed, editorially reviewed or approved       |

This separation is important.

The Context copy is the educational artifact. The visual brief specifies what the interactive environment needs to teach. The implementation review identifies discrepancies between the approved explanation and the existing code.

We should avoid asking the coding agent to infer educational requirements from prose alone.

```
    input_model: Monotonic search predicate
    invariant: Unresolved boundary remains in search interval
    guarantee: First satisfying position or end sentinel
    assumptions:
      - Constant-time indexed access
      - Constant-time predicate evaluation
      - Stable input during execution

  experiments:
    - id: valid-monotonic-input
      objective: Observe search-space elimination
    - id: non-monotonic-input
      objective: Demonstrate loss of correctness guarantee

  integration:
    prerequisites:
      - computational-foundations
    related_concepts:
      - database-indexing
      - ordered-data

  evidence:
    status: requires-review

  implementation:
    status: requires-source-inspection
```

This is an illustrative editorial schema, not a proposed mandatory application data model.

It gives us a precise way to communicate the lesson's intent without prematurely committing to a repository architecture.

## **14. Educational evaluation framework**

Since we have been developing the broader Harness editorial methodology, this track gives us an opportunity to establish more concrete educational evaluations.

Algorithms are particularly suitable for this because we can often test understanding through prediction and counterexamples.

I propose six evaluation dimensions.

| Dimension                  | What we evaluate                                         | Weight |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------ |
| Technical correctness      | Definitions, implementation, assumptions and guarantees  | 25%    |

### **Testing actual understanding**

Editorial scoring is only a proxy for educational quality.

The stronger test is whether a reader can answer a question that differs from the running example.

Consider Binary Search.

A weak assessment asks the reader to state its time complexity.

A stronger assessment gives the reader a new dataset and asks whether binary search can be applied, what property is required, and how they would establish that property.

### **Example transfer assessment**

A monitoring system stores hourly server latency measurements in chronological order. An engineer wants to find the first hour when latency exceeded 200 milliseconds.

Can ordinary boundary-finding binary search reliably locate that hour?

Yes, because the records are chronologically sorted.

Yes, provided there are enough records.

Not generally, because the threshold predicate may not be monotonic.

No, because binary search cannot operate on numerical measurements.

This type of assessment tests whether the reader has acquired a transferable computational principle.

It also gives us a potential foundation for future educational evals of the Harness editorial skill.

## **18. The longer-term opportunity for Harness**

There is a broader opportunity emerging from this work.

The algorithms track could eventually become the intellectual foundation of the entire publication.

A product investigation can lead us downwards towards the computational mechanisms that make it possible. A mathematical investigation can lead us upwards towards the architectures and products that exploit its properties.

This also establishes a meaningful distinction between Harness and the AI Knowledge Hub publication. The publication can investigate emerging products and technologies, while Harness develops the enduring knowledge required to understand them.&#x20;

The goal is not to build an encyclopaedia of algorithms. It is to develop a coherent educational environment in which the reader gradually learns to recognise the same underlying ideas across apparently unrelated technology systems.

**My recommendation is to approve this architecture and proceed with Phase 4.1: the Binary Search pilot.** That will be our first opportunity to test whether the proposed curriculum works at the level that ultimately matters — an individual reader encountering a computational problem and learning to reason about it independently.
