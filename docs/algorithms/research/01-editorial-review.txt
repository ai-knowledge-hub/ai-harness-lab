Yes, I think there is a strong reason to undertake an editorial review of the algorithms module. Potentially, it is a more important exercise than the revisions we have been making to individual system design articles.

With the system design articles, we have been transforming explanations of specific technologies and implementations into transferable lessons about engineering. With algorithms, we have an opportunity to work in the opposite direction: take foundational computer science concepts and demonstrate how they become engineering decisions in real systems.

The two sections should reinforce each other.

I would not want Harness to become another algorithm catalogue or an alternative to LeetCode. There are already excellent resources for learning algorithm implementation, complexity analysis and interview problem-solving. Harness has an opportunity to distinguish itself by teaching **why algorithms exist, what properties make them useful, and how to recognise the problems they solve inside larger systems.**

However, we should preserve the mathematical foundations and standalone educational value of the algorithms themselves. Context should deepen understanding, not replace it.

## **2. A technical finding that demonstrates why the review is necessary**

The existing Binary Search example is particularly revealing.

The lesson describes finding the first date on which a campaign's cost per acquisition crosses a threshold. The visible explanation says that the series is ordered by date, and the code uses binary search to locate the threshold crossing.&#x20;

However, chronological ordering alone is insufficient.

Binary search requires the search predicate to be monotonic: once the threshold condition becomes true, it must remain true throughout the remaining search space.

Daily CPA does not necessarily behave like that.

The algorithm discards the left half after examining Day 4, even though Day 2 already satisfies the condition.

This is not a reason to abandon the lab. It is an excellent teaching opportunity.

We could explain why binary search works on cumulative spend under non-negative additions and a fixed data snapshot, but not necessarily on fluctuating daily performance metrics.

The educational distinction is substantial:

- **Superficial lesson:** Binary search is faster than linear search.
- **First-principles lesson:** Binary search is faster because the structure of the search space allows us to eliminate candidates without inspecting them individually.
- **Engineering lesson:** If the required structure does not exist, the algorithm may return an incorrect result, irrespective of its efficiency.

This is exactly the kind of reasoning Harness should teach.

## **3. Establishing the intellectual identity of the algorithms track**

I would define this track around a central question:

**How does the structure of a problem determine what a computer can do efficiently and reliably?**

That gives the module a stronger intellectual foundation than either algorithm implementation or AI-agent optimisation alone.

Consider three levels of engineering knowledge:

Level 3 · Application

**Systems**

Databases, marketplaces, advertising platforms, distributed systems and AI agents.

Level 2 · Mechanism

**Algorithms and data structures**

Search, traversal, ranking, scheduling, indexing, allocation and state management.

Level 1 · Foundation

**Mathematical and computational principles**

Order, sets, graphs, probability, invariants, complexity and optimisation.

The algorithms track should connect all three levels.

A reader learning about advertising auctions should be able to descend into the relevant algorithms and mathematical principles. A reader learning about heaps should be able to ascend into scheduling, advertising, databases or information retrieval.

This creates a network of knowledge rather than a collection of articles.

It also gives us a practical editorial rule: **every algorithm lesson should explain both the principle beneath it and the systems above it.**

The current selection combines classical algorithms, data structures, distributed coordination mechanisms, security models and control mechanisms.

I would retain them while making the distinctions explicit.

This distinction also exposes several important technical questions.

For example, the current description of the Lease-Locked Queue promises a single executor. A lease alone cannot guarantee that only one worker continues acting: a paused worker might resume after its lease has expired and another worker has acquired ownership.

Likewise, graph traversal can support permission resolution, but RBAC and ABAC are policy models, not graph traversal algorithms. An audit hash chain can help reveal modification, but without a trusted checkpoint or equivalent protection, an attacker with sufficient access may be able to rewrite the chain.

These are not minor wording concerns. They determine whether a reader takes away a correct understanding of a system's guarantees.

## **5. How I would redesign the Context module**

This is where I agree most strongly with your suggestion.

We already have the animation, reference implementation and execution controls. The Context module should become the intellectual companion to that interactive experience.

I would establish a reusable lesson architecture for every mechanism.

**01 — The problem**

Begin with a realistic engineering problem. Establish why a straightforward solution becomes insufficient.

**02 — The underlying principle**

Introduce the mathematical or computational property that makes a better approach possible.

**03 — How the mechanism works**

Explain its state, operations and invariants. Connect the explanation to the existing Run tab.

**04 — The cost of computation**

Derive time and space complexity. Compare alternatives and explain the trade-offs.

**05 — Assumptions and failure conditions**

Establish what the mechanism guarantees, when it applies and when it produces an incorrect or incomplete result.

**06 — Inside real systems**

Examine documented production implementations or open-source code. Distinguish actual implementation from illustrative application.

**07 — Choosing the right mechanism**

Compare related approaches and help readers recognise the same computational problem in unfamiliar domains.

**08 — Experiment and test**

Ask the reader to predict a result, change a constraint or identify a failure before revealing the explanation.

These are editorial components, not necessarily eight visible headings in every lesson. We should avoid making the presentation mechanically repetitive.

Most lessons could be approximately 800–1,500 words, with deeper treatment where the mathematical foundations justify it.

## **6. An example of what the improved learning experience could look like**

Let's take the Heap lab, which is already the recommended entry point to the track.

### **Proposed lesson: How Systems Find the Best Results Without Sorting Everything**

Imagine an advertising platform processing one million campaigns. An operator needs to identify the ten campaigns with the highest spending.

The obvious approach is to collect all one million records, sort them and return the first ten.

But what if the records arrive continuously? What if the system cannot keep all of them in memory? And why should it establish the relative order of a million campaigns when the operator only cares about ten?

The problem presents an opportunity: most of the information required for a complete sort is irrelevant to the requested result.

That is where we introduce partial ordering and the heap.

### **The mathematical principle**

To maintain the ten highest values observed so far, we do not need to know the full order of those ten values after every insertion.

We only need efficient access to the smallest member of the current selection.

If a new value is smaller, we discard it. If it is larger, we replace the smallest candidate and restore the heap property.

The following small experiment demonstrates this.

Notice that the heap does not maintain a fully sorted list. It maintains just enough order to perform the operation we need.

This leads naturally into complexity analysis.

| Approach                    | Worst-case time       | Storage for retained records |
| --------------------------- | --------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Full comparison sort        | O(n log n)            | O(n)                         |
| Sorted list of K candidates | O(nK)                 | O(K)                         |
| Bounded min-heap            | O(n log K), for K ≥ 2 | O(K)                         |

For one million records and ten desired results, the difference between maintaining global order and maintaining a small selection becomes significant.

### **From the algorithm to the system**

We can then investigate how this mechanism relates to real engineering problems:

| Domain              | Application to investigate                                |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Search engines      | Retaining the highest-scoring search candidates           |
| Databases           | Executing bounded ORDER BY and LIMIT queries              |
| Operating systems   | Selecting work through priority queues                    |
| Advertising         | Maintaining the highest-ranked campaign or bid candidates |
| AI engineering      | Ranking retrieval candidates                              |
| Distributed systems | Prioritising queued jobs                                  |

These are application directions, not claims that every system uses this exact heap implementation.

During the evidence phase, we could investigate the actual implementations in PostgreSQL, Lucene and Python's standard library, among other primary sources.

We would then explain where the approach breaks. For example, if campaign scores change after they have been discarded, a simple bounded heap cannot necessarily maintain the correct results without additional state or recomputation.

That adds a temporal dimension to the problem and creates a natural connection to streaming systems.

### **What the reader should take away**

The enduring lesson is not that heaps are useful for marketing.

It is that **maintaining only the information required by an operation can substantially reduce computational cost**.

Once the reader understands this principle, they can recognise opportunities for partial ordering elsewhere.

That is the kind of transformation I would aim for across the entire module.

## **9. Research and evidence standards**

The research methodology for this track should differ slightly from the system design track.

For system design, we often start by investigating real implementations and deriving the underlying engineering principles.

For algorithms, we can start with a formally defined computational problem, establish its properties, and then investigate implementations.

Our core research library could include:

| Reference                       | Role                                              |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Introduction to Algorithms      | Correctness, invariants and complexity analysis   |
| The Art of Computer Programming | Mathematical and historical foundations           |
| Algorithms                      | Implementation and visual explanation             |
| MIT 6.006                       | Undergraduate computational foundations           |
| Original algorithm papers       | Historical origins, formal claims and assumptions |
| Production source code          | Evidence of actual implementation choices         |
| Reproducible experiments        | Behaviour under different inputs and constraints  |

The original papers are particularly interesting from an editorial perspective. Algorithms were developed in response to particular computational problems, often under hardware constraints very different from those we face today.

That history can enrich the explanation where it reveals why a particular idea mattered. It should not become a mandatory historical preamble.

For visual pedagogy, I would draw inspiration from the teaching approaches of 3Blue1Brown, Reducible and visual algorithm textbooks, particularly their ability to make invariants, transformations and mathematical relationships visible.
