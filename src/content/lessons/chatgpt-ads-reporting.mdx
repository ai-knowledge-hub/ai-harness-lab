## Twelve conversions. Then fifteen. Then fourteen.

At 9 a.m., an advertising report says yesterday's campaign generated 12 conversions. At 3 p.m., it says 15. The following morning, it says 14.

Each number may be a valid observation made at a different time. Advertising reports combine records that can arrive late, out of order or more than once. Understanding the result requires knowing what the platform counted and what the client retrieved.

We will follow a fictional retailer advertising a coffee grinder on 21 November 2026. The example uses UTC reporting dates and GBP. **The task is to establish what the report tells us, what may still change, and which decisions the evidence supports.**

## Where the numbers come from

A customer sees an advertisement, clicks it and purchases the grinder that afternoon. The advertising platform records an impression, a tracking service records a click, and the retailer records an order. A browser integration or server-side conversion API then notifies the advertising platform about the purchase.

These systems may have different clocks, identifiers and delivery guarantees. The platform must validate the records, determine which belong together and decide whether the purchase qualifies for attribution. Some purchases may be unobservable or impossible to match.

A conceptual model is:

**Event collection → Validation and identity resolution → Attribution → Aggregation → Reporting API**

These are processing responsibilities, not necessarily separate services. The model does not describe any advertising company's private infrastructure.

### From individual events to reporting rows

An event describes an occurrence; a reporting row contains calculations over a group of events. Our retailer requests daily campaign performance and receives:

| Metric | Reported value |
|---|---:|
| Impressions | 8,000 |
| Clicks | 320 |
| Spend | £160 |
| Attributed conversions | 12 |

*All figures, identifiers and campaign events in this article are hypothetical.*

The account is `retailer-uk`, the campaign is `coffee-grinder`, and the reporting date is 21 November 2026.

<ReportingDiagram kind="event-to-row" />

For this report, the **aggregation key** consists of account, campaign and reporting date. Adding country as a dimension adds country to the key. Two rows for the same campaign and date but different countries are legitimate groups, not duplicates.

Keep three concepts distinct:

- The aggregation key identifies a group within a report.
- The report definition specifies scope, dimensions, metrics and calculation rules, including attribution settings.
- The snapshot identity distinguishes observations collected at different times.

Matching row keys do not make reports comparable if their definitions differ. Ignoring either distinction can overwrite legitimate rows or combine incompatible observations.

### Even a date is a calculation rule

A purchase at 00:30 UTC on 22 November belongs to that date in a UTC account, but may belong to 21 November in an account reporting farther west. Some systems assign conversions to the advertising interaction's date rather than the purchase date. The API contract must establish which rule applies.

Currency also constrains aggregation: combining spend across currencies requires an explicit conversion policy and exchange-rate basis. **Correct arithmetic cannot compensate for an incorrectly defined measurement.**

## Why yesterday's report changes

Assume three additional eligible purchase notifications are processed between our first and second observations. The purchases happened during the relevant period, but the information needed to credit them arrived later.

**Event time** is when the purchase happened. **Processing time** is when a system handles its notification. Consider three purchases:

| Purchase | Event time | Processing time |
|---|---|---|
| A | 21 Nov, 18:10 | 21 Nov, 18:11 |
| B | 21 Nov, 20:15 | 22 Nov, 11:40 |
| C | 21 Nov, 19:05 | 22 Nov, 13:20 |

C happened before B but was processed after it. Network delays, scheduled uploads and integration failures can all make processing order differ from event order.

### The problem with closing a reporting window

At midnight, a system counting purchases by event date can publish immediately or wait for more notifications. Waiting may improve coverage, but delays useful information and does not, without an external guarantee, prove that every event has arrived.

Stream-processing systems manage this trade-off explicitly. In Apache Beam, a *window* groups events into a time interval, a *watermark* estimates progress through event time, and a *trigger* determines when an aggregation result is emitted. Depending on configuration, later information can update a provisional result.

*Source: [Apache Beam: Windowing, watermarks and triggers](https://beam.apache.org/documentation/programming-guide/#windowing).*

### Watch the aggregate change

Our simplified processor counts 12 eligible purchases at 09:00, then processes three more for the same reporting date:

`12 + 3 = 15`

The new aggregate supersedes the earlier observation. This illustrates the temporal problem; it does not establish that an advertising platform uses Beam or this update mechanism.

### Freshness is not finality

Metrics can mature at different rates. Impressions and clicks may become reportable relatively quickly, spend may need reconciliation, and conversions depend on event delivery, matching and attribution. OpenAI's Ads reporting documentation describes distinct update schedules for these metrics.

*Source: [OpenAI: Ads reporting documentation](https://developers.openai.com/ads/reporting).*

A report collected five minutes ago can still contain provisional conversion data. An attribution window defines which outcomes qualify for credit after an interaction; it does not necessarily guarantee that all eligible records have arrived or become immutable. Claim finality only when the platform documents that guarantee.

Later information has raised our count to 15. The next morning, it falls to 14.

## When one purchase becomes two events

The retailer sends browser-side and server-side notifications for the same order. Treating them as independent purchases can count one business event twice. Correct counting therefore depends on identity.

### Messages are not purchases

Retries create a similar problem. If a sender receives no acknowledgement, it cannot tell whether the notification was lost or processed. Retrying improves delivery but may produce a second copy.

A stable business-event identifier can help recognise repeated submissions:

`purchase_id = O-1042`

Two notifications with that identity may describe one purchase. A second purchase, `O-1043`, must remain distinct even if the customer, amount and approximate time match. Deduplicating by those attributes alone could erase legitimate activity.

Meta's recommended browser/server deduplication method matches both event identifier and event name. Those rules belong to Meta's contract; they are not universal matching rules for advertising platforms.

*Source: [Meta: Handling Duplicate Pixel and Conversions API Events](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/ads-commerce/conversions-api/deduplicate-pixel-and-server-events).*

### Why exactly-once processing is not enough

A framework may replay an event after failure while committing its effect on managed state exactly once. Flink documents this guarantee through checkpoint recovery; end-to-end guarantees also depend on the sources and output systems.

*Source: [Apache Flink: Fault tolerance](https://nightlies.apache.org/flink/flink-docs-stable/docs/learn-flink/fault_tolerance/).*

But two different event identifiers can describe the same purchase. Applying each exactly once still counts two conversions. Processing guarantees cannot repair an incorrect business identity.

Three reconciliation problems therefore need different identities. **Event deduplication** recognises records for the same occurrence. **Reporting-row reconciliation** recognises the same aggregate group within one report definition. **Snapshot reconciliation** determines when a newer observation replaces an earlier one.

### Why our report falls from 15 to 14

Assume a duplicate purchase notification was initially counted and later removed:

`15 − 1 = 14`

This correction is hypothetical, not a claim about any provider's production deduplication schedule.

A client collecting both snapshots must not add them. If they describe the same aggregate under the same definition, 14 is the newer observation. Preserve 15 for audit history if useful, but distinguish it from an additional fact to sum. Reconciliation requires the report definition, full aggregation key and observation identity; the latest value still need not be final.

## When the API returns only part of the answer

Our analyst requests campaign performance. The client receives a first page and continuation token, then loses the connection while requesting page two. Its accepted rows can be valid while the requested report remains incomplete. This failure is independent of late source events.

### Pagination does not automatically provide a snapshot

Pagination divides a result into retrievable pieces. A client follows continuation tokens until the API says no pages remain. Whether those pages form a consistent snapshot depends on the documented contract.

Google Ads `Search`, for example, documents cached query results and page tokens; `SearchStream` returns results through a streaming response. Consistency within a cached retrieval does not make the metrics immutable across later queries.

*Sources: [Google Ads API: Page through results](https://developers.google.com/google-ads/api/docs/reporting/paging), [Search and SearchStream](https://developers.google.com/google-ads/api/docs/reporting/overview).*

### Retries must preserve the original question

A timeout alone may not reveal whether a request reached the server or its response was lost. A read-only retry may be appropriate under the API's limits and token rules, but it must preserve the original account, dates, dimensions and attribution configuration.

Use bounded retries, backoff and explicit failure states. If a required page cannot be retrieved, retain accepted rows and mark collection incomplete.

### Pagination and partitioning solve different problems

Pagination divides one query result; partitioning divides a task into smaller queries. A monthly report might use one query per day, each with its own pages. The collector must finish those pages and verify that its partitions cover the intended scope without unintended overlap.

Every submitted query can succeed while a day that was never requested remains missing. Successful API calls alone cannot prove scope coverage.

## Recovery requires more than saving a cursor

After a crash, a useful checkpoint identifies the original query, completed partitions, accepted pages and continuation state. Tokens remain usable only under the API's rules. If one expires or cannot be resumed safely, restart the affected partition and reconcile its replacement observations without double-counting.

<ReportingDiagram kind="two-clocks" />

### Four properties, not one status

Validate a report against four questions:

| Property | Question |
|---|---|
| Retrieval completeness | Did the client retrieve every required page and partition? |
| Scope coverage | Does the result cover the requested accounts, dates, dimensions and metrics? |
| Data maturity | Have the underlying metrics reached a suitable processing state for this use? |
| Decision suitability | Does the evidence support the intended interpretation or action? |

These properties are related, but none guarantees the others. If nine of ten pages contain £900 in compatible, non-overlapping spend rows, £900 is their subtotal, not a verified total for the request. Receiving the final page resolves that retrieval gap without establishing conversion finality or causal impact.

## What the reported conversions actually mean

The collector now holds the complete available report: 14 attributed conversions. Whether advertising caused 14 additional purchases is a different question.

### Attribution assigns credit

Attribution rules determine which observed conversions receive credit for ad interactions. They may specify eligible clicks or impressions, windows, matching information, reporting dates and attribution models.

A customer already planning to buy the grinder might see an ad, click and purchase. The purchase can qualify for attribution even if it would have happened anyway. The report cannot directly observe that alternative outcome.

### Incrementality requires a counterfactual

The causal question is: **how many purchases happened because of the advertising that would not otherwise have happened?** A controlled experiment helps estimate the outcome without the campaign.

Suppose eligible customers are randomly assigned to treatment and control groups. Treatment customers are eligible for the advertising intervention; control customers are withheld from it under the experiment's design. We compare their outcomes:

| Group | Customers | Purchases |
|---|---:|---:|
| Treatment | 10,000 | 120 |
| Control | 10,000 | 100 |

The purchase rates are 1.2% and 1.0%, a difference of 0.2 percentage points. Under appropriate randomisation and measurement assumptions, this difference helps estimate the intervention's incremental effect. It does not mean advertising caused all 120 treatment purchases or that the observed difference is the exact effect. Sampling uncertainty, interference and implementation failures matter.

Google's Conversion Lift documentation provides a production example of treatment/control comparisons for advertising impact.

*Source: [Google Ads: Conversion Lift](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/12003020).*

### A complete report can still support the wrong conclusion

Suppose attributed conversions rise by 20% after a budget increase. Seasonal demand, a price reduction, inventory or competing campaigns could explain the change. The report has not isolated the budget increase's causal effect.

Attributed reporting can support monitoring and operational decisions under explicit assumptions. Claims about additional sales require evidence that addresses the causal question. An agent recommending budget changes must preserve that distinction.

## Building a report another system can safely use

The retailer asks an agent: "Should we increase the coffee-grinder campaign budget tomorrow?" A successful reporting call does not establish that the evidence supports a recommendation. The reporting service needs to expose what it retrieved and the conditions under which the figures can be used.

### Make the guarantees explicit

A proposed consumer-side evidence envelope can include:

- **Scope:** account, period, time zone, currency, dimensions and metric definitions.
- **Collection:** completed pages and partitions, failures and verified coverage.
- **Maturity:** collection time, source freshness and known revision limitations.
- **Provenance:** source API, query identity and observation identifier.
- **Semantics:** attribution settings, reporting grain and missing-value definitions.
- **Limitations:** uncertainty and unsupported conclusions.

This is an application design, not an existing OpenAI Ads response schema. Its purpose is to make the report's properties machine-checkable.

### The same report can be acceptable for one use and rejected for another

A report with all requested pages and correct scope can still contain provisional conversions. A dashboard may display it with a freshness warning, while a budget-change workflow requires more mature metrics, a longer comparison period and additional performance evidence. Suitability depends on the intended use.

<ReportingDiagram kind="decision-boundary" />

### An enforceable decision boundary

Before an agent submits a budget-change proposal, a policy might require:

1. Scope matching the campaign and comparison period.
2. Every required page and partition collected.
3. Available metrics meeting the workflow's maturity policy.
4. A specified decision model with explicit assumptions.
5. Permission to propose the specific action.

Enforce these checks in application logic. A failed condition returns a limitation or requests more evidence. The agent cannot override the policy by producing a persuasive paragraph.

### Recommendation is not execution

Keep four operations distinct:

**Recommendation:** The agent proposes a specific budget change.

**Approval:** An authorised person or policy approves it before execution, with human approval where required.

**Execution:** A separately authorised operation submits the change.

**Verification:** The system checks whether the intended platform state was reached.

A proposal does not establish that the budget changed. A confirmed budget change does not establish that sales improved or that advertising caused the improvement.

## What a trustworthy report actually guarantees

<span id="preserve-uncertainty-rather-than-disguising-it" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />

Our 12 → 15 → 14 sequence exposes several independent guarantees: event identity determines what counts; processing determines when information arrives; report definitions determine what rows mean; and collection validation establishes what the client received. Measurement assumptions then constrain the conclusions.

A useful report carries those boundaries forward. Keep accepted rows with missing coverage, provisional figures with their collection time and revision limits, and unavailable metrics as missing values. A null conversion count must not silently become zero, and an attributed count must not become a claim of incremental sales.

This discipline applies to financial dashboards, marketplace analytics and operational monitoring as well as advertising. **The result should make clear what is known, what may still change, and what the evidence permits us to do next.**
