## A campaign across three clocks

A retailer has £1,000 to advertise a coffee grinder today. It wants customers who would not otherwise have bought one. But there is no marketplace where it can simply purchase those customers. Instead, the retailer buys access to advertising opportunities: a placement beside a search result, a sponsored product position, a slot in a video, or an advertisement within another digital experience. The platform must decide which advertiser gets each opportunity, keep spending within the applicable constraints, and later determine what happened.

The difficulty is that these operations unfold on different clocks. An advertising decision may need to complete almost immediately. Thousands of decisions can compete for the same budget. A purchase may occur days later, and its record may arrive later still. Even when the platform observes a purchase, it cannot infer from that observation alone whether the advertisement caused it.

Advertising platforms therefore solve three connected problems: **allocation, execution under constraints, and measurement from incomplete evidence.** Following one campaign through those problems reveals why their systems are designed as they are.

## You cannot buy a customer

Our retailer creates a campaign with a £1,000 daily budget, a coffee grinder advertisement, and a purchase objective. The platform attempts to match that campaign to suitable *inventory*: opportunities where advertising can appear. A search placement, a social feed position, and a sponsored product listing are all inventory, although their contexts and commercial arrangements differ.

The advertiser wants profitable sales. The inventory owner wants revenue without damaging its service. The person using the service wants to accomplish something, which may have nothing to do with advertising. The platform must allocate opportunities while respecting these competing interests and its own rules.

That makes the advertiser's objective different from what it actually buys. A campaign might be optimised for purchases but charged per click or impression. A click is observable under defined conditions; a profitable incremental purchase is a much harder outcome to establish.

Consider the economics. Suppose a click costs £2 and the retailer earns £60 contribution margin on each additional purchase. For this hypothetical calculation, assume that a paid click produces an incremental purchase probability of 5% relative to a defined counterfactual. This is a causal assumption, not a figure we can obtain simply by dividing observed purchases by clicks. The expected incremental contribution is:

<LessonEquation kind="contribution" />

After the £2 click cost, the expected net contribution is £1 per click, before other relevant costs. But the word *incremental* is doing important work. If 5% is merely the observed conversion rate among people who clicked, some customers might have purchased anyway. The calculation would no longer establish incremental profit.

The retailer must make spending decisions using estimates of future value. Whether those estimates reflect genuine causal impact is a measurement problem we will return to. First, the platform needs to decide which advertisement receives an opportunity.

## Who gets to show the ad?

Someone searches for "best coffee grinder for espresso". Our retailer wants the advertising placement. So do two competitors. The platform cannot give all three advertisers the same position. It needs an allocation rule.

Before ranking them, it determines which campaigns are eligible. A campaign might be paused, lack sufficient budget, fail a targeting condition, or contain an unapproved creative. Only eligible candidates enter the selection process.

### Ranking and pricing are different problems

Imagine a simplified auction that ranks eligible advertisements using:

| Competitor A | £3.00 | 0.5 | 1.50 |
| Competitor B | £1.50 | 0.8 | 1.20 |

This simplified ranking is not a reproduction of a vendor's actual ranking formula. Google's documented Search Ad Rank, for instance, incorporates multiple factors beyond the bid, including quality, thresholds and auction context. Google documents Ad Rank calculations affecting both eligibility and placement; the stages in this teaching example should not be mistaken for Google's internal calculations. <PlatformCitation source="S3" />

But ranking determines only who wins. It does not necessarily determine what the winner pays. Suppose our hypothetical auction charges the minimum bid needed to match the next-highest score. Competitor A scores 1.50. Our retailer's quality factor is 0.9.

<LessonEquation kind="threshold" />

The threshold is approximately £1.67. Under our invented pricing rule, this is the bid needed to match the competing score. Whether matching is sufficient to win depends on the tie-breaking rule; a strictly higher score would require a slightly higher bid. The example illustrates pricing logic, not the actual clearing-price calculation of Google Ads or an OpenRTB exchange.

**The bid, ranking score, winning decision, and charge are separate quantities.** Different auction mechanisms connect them in different ways. The generalised second-price model studied by Edelman, Ostrovsky, and Schwarz is a foundational example, but modern platforms need not use that exact mechanism. <PlatformCitation source="S1" />

Nor is every opportunity auctioned. Inventory can be reserved or sold through negotiated agreements. Google's Programmatic Guaranteed documentation, for example, describes fixed-price deals with committed impression volumes, even though real-time bidding technology can participate in fulfilment. <PlatformCitation source="S5" /> The underlying problem is allocation. Auctions are one family of solutions.

Our retailer has won its placement. But the campaign may win thousands of opportunities today, all competing for the same £1,000 budget. That changes the problem.

## A budget is a constraint across time

By 11 a.m., the retailer's campaign has spent £800. Only £200 remains for the rest of the day. Was that a mistake? If evening shoppers are more likely to purchase, perhaps. But if the morning contained unusually valuable opportunities, saving money for later might have been worse.

The platform must allocate a finite budget across opportunities it cannot know in advance. This is the purpose of *budget pacing*. A simple pacing policy might distribute spending approximately across the day. A more sophisticated one might adapt bids or participation according to opportunity quality, expected demand and remaining budget. Research on repeated auctions with budgets formalises this problem: each decision consumes scarce resources while future opportunities remain uncertain. <PlatformCitation source="S2" />

Pacing helps decide **when to spend**. It does not guarantee that concurrent spending decisions respect the available balance.

### Two workers, £10 remaining

Imagine the campaign has £10 left. Two workers independently evaluate opportunities, each requiring an £8 commitment. Both read the balance before either updates it.

```text
Available: £10

Worker A reads £10
Worker B reads £10

Worker A approves £8
Worker B approves £8

Total commitments: £16
```

Each worker made a locally valid decision. Together, they exceeded the shared constraint. The failure occurs because checking the balance and reserving funds were not one coordinated operation.

A hypothetical implementation could use an atomic reservation: approve £8 only if the available balance is still at least £8, and reduce that balance in the same operation. The first reservation succeeds. The second sees only £2 and fails. This protects the reservation limit in the simplified model, but real advertising accounting is more complicated.

### A win is not necessarily a charge

An auction result may create a potential commitment before the final billable event occurs. A system might reserve capacity, later recognise a charge, and reconcile the two. These are logical accounting responsibilities, not necessarily three separate services.

The distinction is visible in OpenRTB 2.6, which separates a win notice from a billing notice. Winning an auction does not automatically establish that the advertisement was delivered or that expenditure should be charged. OpenRTB bid prices are expressed in CPM units; the per-event pounds in our toy auction are not OpenRTB price fields. <PlatformCitation source="S4" />

That raises another design question: should every advertising decision synchronously contact one authoritative budget counter? Strong coordination can protect shared state, but it adds latency and availability dependencies.

An alternative hypothetical design gives delivery workers bounded spending allowances. They can make local decisions without contacting a central counter for every opportunity, provided the total allocated allowances respect the protected budget.

This reduces coordination on the critical path, but unused allowances may temporarily strand funds, failed workers may retain commitments, and reconciliation becomes more involved.

The trade-off is not simply speed versus correctness. It is where coordination occurs, which invariants must hold, and how the system behaves during failure.

**Pacing distributes spending. Budget enforcement protects a constraint. Billing and reconciliation determine what was actually charged.**

Our retailer's advertisement has passed the allocation and spending controls. Has the customer seen it? Not necessarily.

## Winning an opportunity is not the same as showing an ad

The platform selects our retailer's advertisement and returns the information needed to display it. The response might never reach the browser. The creative might fail to load. The customer might navigate away before it renders. The allocation decision succeeded, but the intended user experience may not have happened. This is why advertising systems distinguish events that appear similar from the outside.

| Event | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Bid submitted | An advertiser offered to participate | That the bid won |
| Auction won | The bid was selected | That the advertisement appeared |
| Creative delivered | Creative material was supplied | That it rendered |
| Impression counted | A defined counting rule was satisfied | That the advertisement was viewable |
| Viewable impression | A specified viewability criterion was met | That someone paid attention |
| Billing event | A charge was recognised under the applicable rules | That advertising caused a purchase |

These are logical distinctions, not a universal sequence. Some advertising environments do not involve bidding, and billing may occur under different conditions. <PlatformCitation source="S4" />

### What does it mean to see an advertisement?

An impression is counted according to the applicable measurement methodology. A viewable impression satisfies additional criteria. For standard display advertising, the commonly used MRC criterion requires at least 50% of an advertisement's pixels to be visible for one continuous second; for qualifying video advertising, the corresponding duration is generally two continuous seconds. Different formats and measurement standards can impose different conditions. <PlatformCitation source="S6" />

These are thresholds for an opportunity to see an advertisement. They are not evidence that a person consciously noticed, understood or remembered it. The distinctions matter because each metric answers a different question.

### When success becomes uncertain

Suppose the delivery service sends an advertisement, but the connection closes before the service receives confirmation. Did the browser receive it? Did it render? The service may not know.

Retrying can help recover from failures, but retries can also duplicate operations or measurement events. Stable identifiers and deduplication rules help systems recognise repeated processing of the same logical event. Their guarantees depend on where identifiers are generated, preserved and checked.

A deduplication mechanism cannot reconstruct an impression that was never observed. Nor does successful delivery establish that the user saw the advertisement.

Our retailer now has a counted impression and a click. The following day, the customer purchases the coffee grinder. The platform must somehow connect that purchase to an advertising interaction that happened earlier. That introduces a different problem: reconstructing events after the fact.

## The events do not arrive in the order the world produced them

On Tuesday morning, the retailer downloads Monday's campaign report. It shows 100 clicks and 8 attributed purchases. On Wednesday, the retailer downloads the same report. Monday now shows 11 purchases. The advertising opportunities from Monday have not changed. The evidence available to the reporting system has.

Consider one customer's journey.

- **Monday, 10:02: Advertising interaction:** The customer clicks the coffee grinder advertisement.
- **Tuesday, 18:00: Conversion:** The customer returns to the retailer's website and purchases the grinder.
- **Wednesday, 02:00: Ingestion:** The purchase record reaches the advertising platform.

The reporting date depends on which event the metric is organised around. A report grouped by advertising-interaction date might associate the conversion with Monday. A report grouped by conversion date might associate it with Tuesday. Wednesday is when the platform received the evidence. Both reports can be correct under their respective definitions.

Google Ads, for example, documents conversion-date reporting alongside metrics associated with advertising interactions. <PlatformCitation source="S7" /> This distinction is particularly important when comparing recent reports or combining data from multiple platforms.

### Why a report changes

Three different processes can revise a previously published number.

A customer may purchase after the advertising interaction. The outcome did not exist when the initial report was produced. A purchase may already have happened, but its record arrives late because of batching, connectivity or processing delays. Or an existing record may be corrected, withdrawn or reclassified.

These are different causes with different recovery requirements. A late conversion is not necessarily a failed ingestion. A delayed record is not necessarily a new purchase. A correction is not necessarily a duplicate.

### Processing evidence that arrives late

Imagine a reporting service aggregating conversion records by campaign and date. If it waits for every possible record before publishing, it may never produce a timely result. If it publishes immediately, the result may exclude later evidence. This is a general stream-processing problem.

The Dataflow model describes the trade-offs involved in processing unbounded, out-of-order data, including correctness, latency and cost. <PlatformCitation source="S8" />

One implementation technique uses event-time windows and *watermarks*: estimates of how far event-time processing has progressed. A watermark helps determine when results should be emitted. It does not prove that no older record will ever arrive.

A system may publish an initial aggregate, revise it as additional evidence arrives, and eventually apply a finalisation policy. Advertising reporting APIs need not expose those internal mechanisms. The externally visible consequence may simply be that recent results are provisional.

<span id="what-to-check-before-using-a-report" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />

To interpret a report, three properties matter: **Freshness** asks when the underlying information was last updated. **Completeness** asks which expected records are included within a defined scope. **Finality** asks whether the reported value can still change.

These properties are independent. A recently updated report may be incomplete. A report that includes every received record may still be missing purchases that were never observed. A finalised metric may remain subject to limits imposed by its measurement methodology.

<PlatformVisual kind="clocks" />

### Duplicate events create another failure mode

Suppose the retailer submits a conversion record. The receiving system stores it, then crashes before acknowledging success. The retailer retries.

Without duplicate detection, the platform might count one purchase twice. A stable conversion identifier can help, provided retries preserve it and duplicate checks are coordinated correctly.

But a processing guarantee applies only within its stated boundary. Exactly-once processing inside a data pipeline does not imply that every real-world purchase was observed exactly once across all participating systems. The distinction becomes even more important when some information cannot be observed or disclosed.

### What the platform knows is not necessarily what it can report

A purchase may be absent from advertising measurement for several reasons. The platform may never have observed it. It may have received the event without enough information to associate it with an advertisement. It may have the information but be restricted from disclosing individual-level records. Or the reporting interface may deliberately expose only privacy-protected aggregates.

Amazon Marketing Cloud provides an example of the latter approach. It supports analysis using pseudonymised signals while applying privacy controls and returning aggregated outputs. <PlatformCitation source="S9" />

The advertiser cannot assume that an API exposes every event available to the platform's internal measurement processes. Nor can it assume that every event the platform observed is eligible for attribution.

This matters when the retailer builds its own reporting integration. If Monday's attributed purchase count changes from 8 to 11, the collector should preserve the original result, the revised result, the retrieval timestamps, the reporting scope and the relevant metric definitions. A complete download does not establish that the reporting period is final. A reliable integration preserves enough evidence to explain what changed and why, rather than silently overwriting yesterday's numbers.

The retailer can now understand how Monday's report gained three purchases. But the report still cannot answer the question that motivated the campaign. Did the advertising generate additional sales?

## A purchase happened. Did the advertisement cause it?

The revised report attributes 11 purchases to Monday's advertising. Perhaps those customers clicked the advertisement before buying. Perhaps some encountered several advertisements across different platforms. The attribution system applies rules to the interactions it can observe.

But imagine a customer who had already decided to buy a grinder. They search for it, click our retailer's advertisement, compare prices and purchase. The advertisement appears in their journey. The purchase follows it.

Would they have bought the grinder anyway? The reporting system cannot answer that question simply by observing the sequence. It sees what happened, not what would have happened to the same customer in an alternative world without the advertisement. That missing alternative is the *counterfactual*.

### Attribution assigns credit

Suppose a customer clicks a search advertisement on Monday, sees a social advertisement on Tuesday, and purchases on Wednesday. A last-touch attribution rule might credit the most recent qualifying advertising interaction. A first-touch rule might credit the earliest. Another method might distribute credit across several interactions. The same observed journey can produce different attributed results without any underlying purchase record changing.

Attribution answers which interactions receive credit under a particular rule. Incrementality asks how outcomes would differ without the advertising treatment. To investigate the latter, we need a different kind of evidence.

### Measuring what would have happened otherwise

Imagine the retailer randomly assigns 20,000 eligible customers to two groups. One group is assigned to an advertising treatment. The other is held out. After the experiment, the retailer observes these hypothetical results:

| Group | Customers | Purchases | Purchase rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Treatment assignment | 10,000 | 300 | 3.0% |
| Control assignment | 10,000 | 250 | 2.5% |

The estimated absolute difference is:

<LessonEquation kind="lift" />

Relative to the control group's purchase rate, that is a 20% lift. Under a simplified model with independent observations, an approximate 95% confidence interval for the absolute difference runs from 0.05 to 0.95 percentage points.

The interval describes uncertainty in the estimated difference under the stated statistical model. It does not establish that every additional purchase was caused by an observed impression, nor does it account for violations of the experimental design's assumptions.

There is also a subtle distinction in what the experiment estimates. Customers assigned to treatment are not necessarily customers who actually saw an advertisement. Some may never encounter an eligible opportunity. Others may see several advertisements. Comparing all customers by their random assignment estimates the effect of **assignment to the treatment**, often called the intention-to-treat effect.

Estimating the effect of actual exposure requires further assumptions or an appropriate experimental design. Exposure itself may be associated with behaviour: people who browse more frequently might both encounter more advertisements and purchase more often. Random assignment helps address confounding for the assigned treatment. It does not automatically make every subsequent exposure comparison causal.

Research by Lewis and Rao demonstrates why precise advertising-effect measurement can remain difficult even with large experiments. The effect of advertising may be small relative to the natural variation in purchasing behaviour. <PlatformCitation source="S10" />

<PlatformVisual kind="incrementality" />

### Attributed purchases are not incremental purchases

Suppose a platform attributes 300 purchases to the campaign. A randomised experiment might estimate that the advertising treatment produced 50 additional purchases compared with the control condition. These results need not contradict one another. The first assigns credit to observed purchases. The second estimates a causal difference under the experiment's design and assumptions.

That distinction also explains why attributed conversions cannot necessarily be added across platforms. The same purchase might qualify for attribution on both a search platform and a social platform. Each could apply its own rules correctly while counting the same underlying business outcome.

The retailer's real objective is incremental profit, not the largest possible attributed conversion count. Advertising reports help it operate campaigns. Causal measurement helps it evaluate whether those campaigns create additional value. The two are related, but neither replaces the other.

## Why one platform exposes several interfaces

Our retailer now wants to automate its advertising operations. It needs to create campaigns, adjust budgets, retrieve reports and reconcile advertising expenditure with sales. Why might one platform expose different APIs for those tasks? Because the operations have different workloads and guarantees.

Campaign configuration manages relatively durable business objects: campaigns, creatives, targeting rules and budgets. Ad delivery evaluates opportunities under latency constraints. Conversion ingestion receives business events, sometimes long after the advertising interaction. Reporting processes recorded data into aggregates, often over large historical periods.

A useful distinction is between a *control plane*, which manages the rules under which a system operates, and an *execution path*, which applies those rules to individual opportunities. Changing a campaign budget is a configuration operation. Deciding whether the campaign can participate in an advertising opportunity is an execution decision. That difference becomes important during failure or propagation delays.

### A successful pause request

Suppose the retailer pauses its campaign through an API. The API returns success. Does that prove no further advertising expenditure can occur? Not without a documented guarantee.

The response might confirm that the request was accepted or that a configuration state changed. Advertising decisions already in progress may have been evaluated earlier. Billing events may arrive later. The integration needs to understand what the response actually establishes.

Google's Display & Video 360 interfaces provide a documented example of distinct external responsibilities: campaign management and certain reporting operations use different APIs. <PlatformCitation source="S11" /> That is evidence of interface separation, not proof that Google's internal infrastructure follows identical service boundaries.

### Reporting may require a separate workflow

A report covering millions of events may require substantial computation. Rather than complete everything inside one synchronous request, an API may expose an asynchronous reporting job. The client requests the report, receives a job identifier, checks its status, and retrieves the results when processing finishes. This is a general engineering pattern, not a universal advertising API contract.

It also introduces several independent failure points. The job can be created successfully but fail during computation. The computation can finish while the download fails. A paginated download can return only part of the result. And even a fully retrieved report may remain provisional because late conversions have not yet arrived. A reliable collector therefore needs to track the complete operation, not merely individual HTTP responses.

Suppose the retailer downloads four of five report pages before losing its connection. Appending all five pages on retry could duplicate the first four. A better design might stage the collection under a stable run identifier and publish the dataset only after verifying the expected scope. The exact solution depends on the platform's pagination, snapshot and retry guarantees.

This is where the general principles of API design and distributed systems become practical: **a successful request, a complete dataset and a verified business outcome are different claims.** The API contract determines which of them the software can establish.

## What changes when the advertising environment changes?

<span id="first-platform" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="first-platform-title" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />

The retailer can advertise its coffee grinder through search, social media, retail marketplaces, programmatic inventory or a conversational service. The commercial objective remains the same. The opportunities and available evidence do not.

A search placement may be associated with an explicit query. A social placement appears within a feed or video where the person may not be shopping. A retail marketplace may connect advertising activity with purchases inside its commerce environment. Programmatic systems coordinate opportunities across participating publishers and intermediaries.

A conversational interface introduces another context: the user may be exploring a question, comparing alternatives or working through a task. Each environment changes what the platform knows, which advertising opportunities exist and how outcomes can be observed.

| Environment | Opportunity | Important system questions |
|---|---|---|
| Search | Search-related placement | How do query relevance, eligibility and ranking interact? |
| Social | Placement within social content | Which signals inform selection and downstream measurement? |
| Retail media | Shopping or related media placement | Which purchases are observable and attributable? |
| Programmatic | Opportunity exposed through participating inventory systems | Which transaction, delivery and billing rules apply? |
| Conversational | Placement associated with a conversation or task | How is advertising separated from the assistant's response, and what can be measured? |

These are categories, not uniform implementations. Each platform's documented contracts determine the actual behaviour.

### Applying the model to an unfamiliar platform

Suppose the retailer discovers a new advertising service promising better purchase performance through automated optimisation. The obvious starting point might be its campaign creation API. But an endpoint tells us which operation software can request. It does not, by itself, explain the underlying system.

A more useful investigation begins with the opportunity. What inventory is available? What makes a campaign eligible? How is allocation performed? What event creates a charge? Which delivery outcomes can be verified? What can the platform observe, and which information can it disclose?

Only then should the retailer examine the configuration and reporting interfaces.

A field named `conversions` may count different qualifying events across platforms. A daily budget may have different enforcement semantics. A successful pause request may have different propagation guarantees. Matching field names do not establish matching contracts.

### Two platforms, two reports

The retailer spends £500 on Platform A and £500 on Platform B. Platform A reports 40 attributed purchases. Platform B reports 55. Is Platform B better? Not necessarily.

Perhaps its attribution window is longer. Perhaps the platforms observe different subsets of purchases. Perhaps some customers interacted with advertisements on both, allowing the same purchase to receive credit twice. Even if both reports are correct under their respective definitions, the totals may not be directly comparable. And neither number establishes incremental profit. The retailer needs to reconcile the metric definitions, observation boundaries, reporting periods and business outcomes before drawing a conclusion.

That is the advantage of understanding the system beneath the interface. Instead of treating a report as a collection of numbers, we can trace each metric back through the decisions, events and rules that produced it.

## Decisions first, evidence later

The platform cannot wait to learn whether a customer will purchase before deciding which advertisement to show. It must act using estimates and incomplete information. It must coordinate spending while decisions happen concurrently. Later, it reconstructs what happened from events that may be delayed, duplicated, corrected or unavailable.

This creates an asymmetry at the heart of advertising systems. **Decisions happen before outcomes are known. Measurement reconstructs the available evidence afterward.**

An allocation can succeed without producing an impression. An impression can be counted without establishing attention. A purchase can be attributed without proving causality. The system's reliability depends partly on preserving those boundaries rather than hiding them.

Once we can distinguish the decision from its execution, the observation from its interpretation, and the attributed outcome from the causal effect, an unfamiliar advertising platform becomes easier to investigate. Not because we know its internals, but because we know which questions its architecture must answer.
