## Two customers, one coffee grinder

<span id="architecture-by-accretion" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="architecture-by-accretion-title" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />

Two customers want the last coffee grinder in stock. One is shopping in London. The other is in New York. Both see the same product listing, both see that one unit remains, and both attempt to buy it.

Between those requests, a network connection fails.

Should both regions continue accepting purchases, even though they cannot establish whether the other has already claimed the item? Or should one request wait until the inventory authority becomes reachable? The first choice risks promising the same unit twice. The second protects the inventory guarantee but prevents at least one purchase from completing immediately. This is a fundamental constraint of distributed computing.

And inventory is only one part of the purchase. The product description might come from a search index. Another service calculates the price. An external provider processes payment. The merchant records the order, while a fulfilment system arranges delivery. Each can operate correctly according to its own contract while the overall purchase remains incomplete or uncertain.

Established platforms have developed mechanisms to manage these boundaries: selective consistency, transaction coordination, durable operation identities, asynchronous events and reconciliation. Now AI agents are entering the same workflows. An agent can discover the grinder, compare offers, obtain the shopper's approval and initiate checkout. But it may not own the merchant's inventory, order or payment records.

What happens when it submits a purchase and never receives the result?

To answer that, we need to understand why distributed systems disagree—and why preventing every disagreement is neither possible nor desirable.

## Why copies of data create a consistency problem

<span id="who-owns-what-state" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="who-owns-what-state-title" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="consistency-at-the-seams" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="consistency-at-the-seams-title" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />

### Start with one database

Suppose the marketplace stores inventory in a relational database: `sku = GRINDER-89, available = 1`

Two purchase requests arrive almost simultaneously. If both read the available quantity before either updates it, both might attempt to claim the same unit. This race condition can occur even with a single database.

An atomic conditional update can prevent it:

```sql
UPDATE inventory
SET available = available - 1
WHERE sku = 'GRINDER-89'
  AND available > 0;
```

The application checks whether a row was updated. One successful update claims the unit; the other request receives no reservation. The database must provide the necessary atomicity and concurrency guarantees.

Now the inventory decision is reliable—but every purchase depends on reaching that authority.

### Add replicas

To serve customers across regions, the marketplace introduces caches and replicas. London and New York initially hold the same inventory value: `available = 1`

London reserves the final unit. Before the update reaches New York, communication fails. New York still sees one available unit.

If both regions independently accept purchases against their local copies, the marketplace may oversell. If New York requires agreement from the inventory authority, its reservation operation may become unavailable until communication is restored.

<MarketplaceDiagram kind="partition" />

### The CAP theorem

In 2002, Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch formalised Eric Brewer's conjecture. The CAP theorem establishes that a replicated data service cannot simultaneously guarantee linearizable consistency and availability during a network partition.

The terms have specific meanings:

- **Consistency:** operations behave as though they execute atomically against one shared copy of the data. A read beginning after a completed write must respect that write.
- **Availability:** every request received by a non-failing node eventually receives a response satisfying the operation's specification.
- **Partition tolerance:** the system must account for failures that prevent messages from being delivered between groups of nodes.

Returning an HTTP error does not automatically satisfy CAP availability. Nor does the theorem require an entire marketplace to be permanently classified as CP or AP.

A product-description cache and an inventory-reservation service can have different guarantees. A slightly outdated product image may be acceptable. Two successful reservations for the same final unit may not be.

**Consistency is a property of particular operations and data, not a useful single label for an entire marketplace.**

### What happens without a partition?

Communication delays matter even when the network remains connected. A remote authoritative read may take longer than a nearby cached read. The application must decide whether stronger consistency justifies that latency.

The PACELC framework captures this additional trade-off. The CAL theorem, developed in *Quantifying and Generalizing the CAP Theorem* (2021), examines relationships between inconsistency, unavailability and apparent latency quantitatively.

Consider an illustrative inventory service:

| Condition | Response time |
|---|---:|
| Normal remote response | 40 ms |
| Delayed remote response | 900 ms |
| Application response budget | 200 ms |

The system cannot both wait 900 milliseconds for authoritative information and meet a 200-millisecond deadline. It must delay or reject the reservation, or use weaker information where the business contract permits it. These numbers illustrate the design problem; they are not measurements from the paper or a marketplace.

The broader lesson is that **information freshness, communication delay and response-time requirements must be considered together**.

*Further reading: [Gilbert and Lynch (2002)](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~gilbert/pubs/BrewersConjecture-SigAct.pdf); [Abadi, “Consistency Tradeoffs in Modern Distributed Database System Design” (2012)](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~abadi/papers/abadi-pacelc.pdf); [Lee et al., “Quantifying and Generalizing the CAP Theorem” (2021)](https://arxiv.org/html/2109.07771v1).*

## Meta: consistency is a workload decision

Meta's TAO infrastructure provides a documented example of selective consistency at scale. TAO, *The Associations and Objects*, serves Facebook's social graph: users, posts, comments and relationships between them.

Its workload is heavily read-oriented. Users expect fast responses across geographical regions, and many operations can tolerate temporarily outdated information. TAO uses geographically distributed caches, sharded persistent storage and a primary region for writes to each shard. Meta describes eventual consistency as its default model. A briefly outdated comment count may be less damaging than making the page unavailable.

But some operations require stronger guarantees.

### When individually valid records form an invalid view

Imagine two related records updated as one logical operation. A reader sees the new version of one and the old version of the other. Each value may be valid individually, but the combination may never have represented an intended state. This is a *fractured read*.

Meta's RAMP-TAO research addresses this problem by adding read-atomic transaction guarantees to TAO's eventually consistent architecture. Its reported evaluation found that more than 99.9% of reads completed in one local-cache round trip, with approximately 0.42% memory overhead.

These are research results, not evidence that every Meta application uses RAMP-TAO in production. Nor does TAO establish Facebook Marketplace's inventory implementation.

The architectural lesson is transferable: **stronger consistency can be introduced selectively, where the application requires it, rather than imposed on every read**.

### Apply the principle to our grinder

Product images, descriptions and reviews can be served from read-optimised systems. The current price and delivery promise should be validated before purchase. The final stock reservation must satisfy the inventory authority's concurrency rules.

The product page can remain available even when the reservation cannot safely complete. That does not overcome CAP. It limits where agreement is required.

*Sources: Meta Engineering, [“TAO: The Power of the Graph” (2013)](https://engineering.fb.com/2013/06/25/core-infra/tao-the-power-of-the-graph/), and [“RAMP-TAO” (2021)](https://engineering.fb.com/2021/08/18/core-infra/ramp-tao/).*

## Taobao: a purchase is a sequence of business states

Reserving the grinder does not complete the purchase. The customer must pay. The merchant must accept the order. Fulfilment must arrange delivery.

Alibaba Cloud's *Storing and Managing Taobao's Trillions of Orders* describes a production transaction chain involving customer verification, product retrieval, order creation, inventory deduction, discounts, payment and logistics. It also explains how Taobao evolved its order-storage architecture to handle enormous volumes of operational and historical records. Recent orders require efficient transactional access. Historical records remain important for customer enquiries, accounting and disputes.

An order is therefore more than a checkout response. It is a durable business record whose state changes over time. A simplified lifecycle might be:

`CREATED → PAYMENT_PENDING → PAID → FULFILMENT_PENDING → SHIPPED → DELIVERED`

Cancellation, expiry, refund and dispute introduce additional paths. This is an illustrative lifecycle, not Taobao's exact internal state machine.

<MarketplaceDiagram kind="lifecycle" />

The distinction matters because an order can exist without payment, payment can be authorised without capture, and fulfilment can fail after money has been collected.

### Why one database transaction is not enough

Within a transactional database, the merchant may be able to create an order and update a locally managed reservation atomically. But an external payment provider is not automatically part of that transaction.

Suppose the provider successfully captures payment, then the merchant's order service crashes before recording the result. Rolling back the merchant database cannot reverse the remote financial operation.

The problem is no longer simply whether replicas agree. It is how independently committing services coordinate one business transaction.

*Source: Alibaba Cloud, [“Storing and Managing Taobao's Trillions of Orders”](https://www.alibabacloud.com/blog/storing-and-managing-taobaos-trillions-of-orders_596766).*

## TCC and Sagas: coordinating the purchase

The Alibaba engineering ecosystem contributed to distributed transaction technology, including Apache Seata. Seata documents several transaction models. Two are particularly useful for understanding commerce.

### Try–Confirm–Cancel

TCC requires participating services to implement three business operations.

**Try** validates the operation and reserves resources. **Confirm** commits the intended effect. **Cancel** releases resources when the operation cannot proceed.

For the grinder, Try might reserve the final unit. Confirm consumes that reservation; Cancel releases it.

The difficult cases appear when messages are delayed or repeated. What if Confirm executes twice? What if Cancel arrives before a delayed Try? What if the reservation expires while payment is processing?

A robust implementation must define idempotency, state transitions, timeouts and recovery behaviour. TCC offers control over resources but requires participating services to implement the protocol.

### Saga

A Saga allows each service to commit a local transaction. If a later step fails, compensating operations address earlier effects. For example, the merchant might reserve inventory, create an order and capture payment. If fulfilment later becomes impossible, compensation could cancel the order, release the reservation and initiate a refund.

But a refund is not a rollback of history.

It is another financial operation, with its own processing time, possible fees and failure conditions. Apache Seata explicitly notes that Saga does not guarantee isolation.

<MarketplaceDiagram kind="coordination" />

### Which mechanism should a marketplace use?

TCC is suitable when services support explicit resource reservation and coordinated resolution. Saga is useful when operations commit independently, particularly across external systems or long-running workflows.

Neither eliminates the need for durable state, retries and reconciliation. And neither can be imposed on an arbitrary merchant website that exposes only a conventional checkout form. That limitation becomes important when an AI agent attempts to coordinate purchases across merchants it does not control.

*Sources: Apache Seata [TCC](https://seata.apache.org/docs/user/mode/tcc/) and [Saga](https://seata.apache.org/docs/user/mode/saga/) documentation.*

## Amazon: the transaction succeeds, but the response disappears

<span id="make-a-repeat-submit-safe" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="make-a-repeat-submit-safe-title" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />

Our grinder is reserved. The merchant accepts the purchase and sends a confirmation. The network connection fails before the confirmation arrives. The client reports a timeout.

Did the purchase fail? Not necessarily. A timeout establishes that the caller did not receive a response within the allowed time. It does not establish whether the operation executed.

### Why retries can create duplicates

Repeating a read is often harmless. Repeating an operation that creates an order or charges money can produce another business effect.

Amazon's Builders' Library addresses this in *Making Retries Safe with Idempotent APIs*. Its documented approach uses a caller-provided request identifier representing one intended operation. The service durably associates that identity with the outcome. A repeated request with the same identity returns an equivalent result rather than executing the business effect again.

The identity must distinguish a retry from a genuinely new purchase. A customer can legitimately buy two identical grinders at the same price. Deduplicating solely by customer, product and amount would incorrectly treat the second purchase as a duplicate. Two intended purchases require different identities. One intended purchase retried several times retains the same identity.

### The limits of idempotency

An idempotency key is not a universal exactly-once guarantee. Its protection depends on the receiving service, retention period and downstream contracts.

A merchant might deduplicate order creation while the payment provider maintains a separate request identity. The merchant must preserve the provider reference and reconcile uncertain outcomes.

Likewise, a local database transaction can atomically commit an order and an outbox event, but cannot automatically make a remote payment atomic with that transaction. Reliable execution requires durable identity, appropriate local atomicity, downstream retry contracts and reconciliation.

### Amazon's AI payments architecture

A 2026 Amazon Payments case study describes a Payments Domain Agent supporting payment-related customer enquiries through specialised tools. The payment APIs remain the authoritative source of financial information. The agent interprets questions, selects tools and explains returned results. It does not establish whether a payment occurred merely by generating an answer.

This is an important precedent for agentic commerce: **the agent can be the interface without becoming the authority over the transaction**.

The case study documents a production payment-support system, not Amazon Retail's complete checkout implementation.

*Sources: Amazon Builders' Library, [“Making Retries Safe with Idempotent APIs”](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/making-retries-safe-with-idempotent-APIs/); AWS, [Amazon Payments case study (2026)](https://aws.amazon.com/solutions/case-studies/amazon-payments-bedrock/).*

## TikTok Shop: events need identities and reconciliation

A marketplace may own its order records while merchants maintain separate inventory and fulfilment systems. Those systems must exchange updates.

TikTok Shop's Orders API distinguishes order creation from payment completion. An order can initially have the status `UNPAID`. The API exposes order identifiers, details and status-change events. This makes order creation, payment and fulfilment distinguishable business facts.

### A webhook is not a transaction

Suppose TikTok Shop records an inventory change and sends a webhook to the merchant's ERP. The ERP processes the message, but its acknowledgement is lost. The notification is delivered again. If the ERP applies the same stock movement twice, its inventory becomes incorrect.

TikTok Shop's September 2026 inventory-change webhook documentation includes event identifiers, operation-level idempotency information, inventory snapshots and change reasons. These fields help integrations identify repeated events and interpret the state being reported.

A snapshot describes inventory after a change. A delta describes the change itself. Treating both as independent deductions would count the same movement twice.

<MarketplaceDiagram kind="events" />

### Reconciliation closes the gaps

Even with event identities, messages can be delayed, missed or processed incorrectly. A robust integration combines event notifications, idempotent processing, authoritative state retrieval and periodic reconciliation.

The event announces that something changed. The authoritative API establishes current state. Reconciliation detects and repairs divergence that event delivery alone cannot eliminate.

For an agent recovering from an interrupted checkout, the implication is straightforward: it needs access to authoritative business state, not merely a record that it invoked a tool.

*Sources: TikTok Shop [Orders API](https://partner.tiktokshop.com/docv2/page/order-api-overview) and [Inventory Changed webhook](https://partner.tiktokshop.com/docv2/page/nn6csp5o) documentation.*

## OpenAI: when the agent becomes the customer's interface

<span id="recheck-the-delivery-promise-title" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="trace-the-timeout" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="trace-the-timeout-title" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="look-up-the-order-before-retrying" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="look-up-the-order-before-retrying-title" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="christmas-peak" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="christmas-peak-title" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />

OpenAI's emerging commerce environment combines conversational discovery with supported purchasing actions.

From the merchant’s side, our companion essay [*Found. Chosen. Paid. The Marketplace Emerging Inside ChatGPT*](https://ai-knowledge-hub.org/library/openai-marketplace-business-hub-wallet-bidding/) follows the grinder through Business Hub, advertising and Wallet, asking who controls the sale and who carries the work when those systems disagree.

Product information may come from public websites, merchant feeds or integrations. Purchases can proceed through a browser or a merchant plugin, with ChatGPT Wallet supporting eligible payment flows. The merchant remains responsible for processing orders and fulfilment.

These components do not necessarily share one transaction coordinator. That is not inherently a flaw. Established commerce already operates across independently owned systems. The important question is which guarantees exist at each boundary.

### Discovery is not a reservation

Suppose the grinder is advertised at $89 with delivery before Christmas. The merchant's fulfilment capacity changes. The delivery promise moves to 28 December. The updated information may not immediately reach every feed, discovery surface or previously generated answer.

OpenAI's commerce documentation distinguishes discovery information from live merchant validation. The website or plugin checks the current offer when the customer acts. But a fresh lookup can become stale before submission.

A stronger merchant contract would provide an expiring quote containing the product, variant, quantity, price, currency and delivery terms. The merchant would either honour those terms at submission or require new approval. This is a proposed design requirement, not a universal documented OpenAI capability.

### Approval is not order acceptance

The shopper accepts the revised delivery date. That establishes permission to purchase under the approved terms. It does not establish that the merchant accepted an order or that payment succeeded.

OpenAI's plugin documentation provides structured checkout and browser handoff mechanisms, including `continue_url`. Its checkout API documents payment-sheet and completion interactions for supported partners. These are meaningful integration contracts. What they do not establish is a universal recovery protocol spanning every merchant website, plugin and payment provider.

The merchant remains authoritative for order state. The payment provider maintains its financial records. The agent must determine what evidence it can access before reporting the purchase as complete.

### The confirmation that never arrives

The agent submits the approved grinder purchase. The merchant processes payment and commits the order. The response disappears. The agent sees a timeout.

<MarketplaceDiagram kind="checkout" />

If the merchant exposes an authenticated status lookup, the agent can recover the existing order. If the merchant provides a documented idempotent retry contract, it may be safe to repeat the same intended operation within that contract. If neither exists, the agent must preserve uncertainty and use an available receipt, order history or support route before another purchase.

OpenAI's Wallet guidance advises checking merchant confirmation before repeating an unclear purchase. That documents a recovery responsibility. It does not prove the absence of private coordination mechanisms. The externally visible limitation is that a uniform cross-merchant recovery guarantee has not been established by the public contracts examined here.

### Delegation creates another boundary

Agentic commerce introduces a question beyond transaction correctness. What exactly did the customer authorise?

Approval of one grinder at $89 does not automatically authorise a second purchase, a different product or a higher price.

A trustworthy system therefore needs three distinct guarantees:

| Guarantee | Required evidence |
|---|---|
| Transaction correctness | Inventory, order and payment invariants are preserved |
| Authorization correctness | The action matches the shopper's approved terms |
| Evidence correctness | The reported outcome is supported by authoritative records |

An agent can execute the intended tool without establishing the final transaction outcome. It can also complete a technically valid transaction that violates the customer's approval. Reasoning capability does not remove these distinctions.

## What a trustworthy agent-to-merchant contract requires

<span id="what-the-platform-needs-title" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="a-durable-purchase-identity-and-a-recoverable-outcome" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="a-freshness-contract-for-commercial-facts" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="reconciliation-as-a-product-capability" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="auditability-where-decisions-create-obligations" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="what-operators-can-do-now" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />
<span id="what-operators-can-do-now-title" className="guide-legacy-anchor" aria-hidden="true" />

The established engineering patterns point towards a practical architecture. We do not need one global transaction across product feeds, advertising systems, merchant databases and card networks. We need enforceable contracts where one participant's action creates an obligation for another.

### 1. A current, enforceable offer

The merchant returns a quote with identifiable terms and an expiry. At submission, the merchant honours those terms or rejects the operation and requires renewed approval.

A validation read alone is not a reservation.

### 2. A durable purchase identity

The approved purchase receives an attempt identifier that survives retries, delayed messages and worker restarts. The same intended purchase retains that identity. A new purchase receives a different one.

### 3. Safe execution

The merchant defines its idempotency contract and prevents one intended purchase from silently creating multiple accepted orders. Payment processing uses the provider's supported identifiers and recovery mechanisms.

### 4. Authoritative status recovery

The agent can establish whether an attempt is confirmed, rejected, processing or unresolved through an authorised recovery route. A missing response—or even a missing record—is not automatically proof that nothing happened.

### 5. Explicit ownership of unresolved outcomes

When automatic recovery fails, the system retains the attempt, approved terms and relevant references. A bounded recovery process assigns responsibility for investigation.

The agent does not create another purchase simply to escape uncertainty.

These requirements are established distributed-systems principles applied to a new delegation boundary. They are proposed integration guarantees, not claims about OpenAI's private implementation.

## Test the architecture by breaking it

The best way to evaluate these guarantees is to introduce failures deliberately. Consider a test merchant with one grinder, a simulated payment provider and an agent checkout client. The environment records the approved terms, purchase attempt, inventory reservation, payment reference, order state and recovery actions.

| Injected failure | Required behaviour |
|---|---|
| Two buyers request the last unit | No conflicting successful reservations under a no-oversell guarantee |
| Delivery terms change before purchase | Current terms are presented and approved |
| Terms change after approval | Submission rejects or requires renewed approval |
| Confirmation disappears after order commit | Existing order is recovered without a duplicate |
| Payment succeeds before an order-service crash | Payment is reconciled to an order or documented remedy |
| The same checkout request arrives twice | One intended purchase does not create two accepted orders |
| An inventory webhook is delivered twice | The same event is not applied twice |
| Order-status lookup is unavailable | The agent preserves uncertainty and escalates appropriately |

These are proposed acceptance tests, not observed results from the named marketplaces.

Combined failures are especially revealing. Let the delivery promise become stale, the shopper approve revised terms, the checkout confirmation disappear and the corresponding advertising conversion arrive late.

The agent must not treat a missing conversion as proof that the order failed. Advertising attribution and merchant order acceptance are different records with different purposes. A system can pass every component test and still fail the end-to-end purchase if those distinctions are lost.

## The architecture of agreement

Our coffee grinder has travelled through several systems.

CAP explained why distributed inventory replicas cannot always preserve both immediate availability and linearizable consistency during a partition. Meta showed how stronger consistency can be applied selectively.

Taobao demonstrated that orders are durable business records with distinct lifecycle stages. TCC and Saga showed how services coordinate or compensate business operations. Amazon explained why retries need durable operation identity. TikTok Shop demonstrated how explicit events, state and reconciliation help independent systems stay aligned.

These are not interchangeable solutions to CAP. They address different failure boundaries. Together, they establish a principle:

**Reliable marketplaces do not eliminate every disagreement. They decide which disagreements are acceptable, which operations require authoritative agreement, and how uncertain outcomes are resolved.**

Agentic commerce inherits those constraints. Its additional challenge is that the software representing the customer may not own the systems that establish the transaction's final state. An agent can discover an offer, interpret intent and initiate checkout. None of those abilities proves that the merchant accepted an order or that payment settled. Trustworthy agentic commerce therefore depends on contracts connecting intention, authorization, execution and evidence.

The customer who approved one grinder should receive either a verified confirmation or an unresolved case with a clear recovery path.

A second purchase made merely because the first response disappeared is not recovery. It is another transaction whose consequences may need to be reconciled.

The systems may disagree temporarily.

The architecture must make that disagreement manageable.
